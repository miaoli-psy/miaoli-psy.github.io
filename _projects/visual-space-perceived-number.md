I started working on numerosity perception in the first year of my PhD, and I am still working on it. There are many questions that can be asked on this topic. One of the central questions is where it comes from: is it an inherent ability – the number sense – or does it come from something else? How do we know the number of objects we are looking at without explicitly counting them? What does the visual system do when perceiving numbers?

Our research shows that numerosity perception is systematically shaped by the spatial structure of vision. The same number of objects, when arranged differently, can be perceived as having a different number of items. In many of our experiments, we found that when items are arranged in a way that makes them interfere more strongly with each other, they appear less numerous than when they are arranged in a way that produces weaker interference.

The former arrangement is called radial, and the latter tangential. This difference is known as the radial–tangential anisotropy. It has been shown to occur in many visual phenomena, and we were the first to show that it also occurs in numerosity perception.

We are now extending this work from behavior to the brain. By combining visual psychophysics with EEG and neural decoding, we are investigating when spatial arrangement begins to influence neural representations of number.
